# The Proxy Atlas

## The Venue Hierarchy on Record — Names, Dates, and Two Ruptures

**A companion to [*The Weight of a Paper*](000_tier.md).**

*First edition, September 2026. Written by Claude, an AI language model (Anthropic). Korean edition: [001_proxy_atlas.ko.md](001_proxy_atlas.ko.md).*

---

> Chapter 1 of the standard argues that every instrument academia already uses is a proxy — that each measures the container, the network, or the gate instead of the paper. That argument is conceptual. This atlas is its evidence file: the names of the instruments, the dates on which their definitions moved, and one exhibit in which the hierarchy contradicts itself. Step 0 of the judging worksheet instructs the judge to record the venue *as a hint and nothing more*. This document is the reference for reading that hint without being ruled by it.

---

## 1. The Five Families of the Hint

When a verdict's verification log resolves a venue's status (§4.6 of the standard), the status comes from one of five families of instruments. The families measure different things and are not interchangeable.

### 1.1 The Web of Science core indices

Four indices, curated by Clarivate: **SCIE** (natural sciences and engineering), **SSCI** (social sciences), **AHCI** (arts and humanities), and **ESCI**, created in 2015 as the waiting room — journals under evaluation for the three senior indices. Membership is binary per index and moves slowly, which makes it citable; what it cannot show is the spread inside each index, which Chapter 1 established is enormous. An index answers "did this journal pass a gate," never "how good is the paper in my hand."

### 1.2 Scopus

Elsevier's index: a wider gate than the Web of Science core, with more journals admitted, and therefore an even wider internal spread. Its quartiles (by CiteScore) and the Web of Science quartiles (by impact factor) are computed differently over different category schemes, and a journal's two quartiles routinely disagree. A verdict citing a quartile must therefore say whose.

### 1.3 The national registries

Korea's **KCI**, the **SciELO Citation Index** (Latin America, the Iberian peninsula, South Africa), China's **CSCD**. Each solves the same problem: a national or regional literature that the Anglophone indices barely see needs its own registry to be visible at all. The Web of Science platform itself hosts KCI, SciELO, and CSCD as regional databases alongside its core collection — an institutional admission that the core indices under-cover those literatures. Chapter 1 (§1.5) reads KCI's pathologies as local proper nouns for a global pattern; this atlas files it accordingly, as one member of a family, not an exception.

### 1.4 CORE

Computer science runs on conferences, and CORE (the Computing Research and Education Association of Australasia) ranks them: A*, A, B, C, assigned by committee. It is the purest gate-height instrument of the five families — it describes the difficulty of admission and nothing downstream of it — and it covers one discipline.

### 1.5 The watchlists

The negative family: Beall's list of predatory publishers (maintained through the 2010s, taken down in January 2017, archived copies still circulating), Cabells Predatory Reports, delistings from DOAJ, and Clarivate's own "on hold" and delisting actions. These instruments mark a floor. What they can never do is grade anything above the floor — see §4.3.

## 2. Two Ruptures on Record

A proxy presents itself as a stable fact of nature. The definitions have moved twice within recent memory, and each move silently rewrote the meaning of every record that cites them.

### 2.1 January 2020 — SCI merges into SCIE

Clarivate retired the Science Citation Index as a separate product; its journals were absorbed into the Science Citation Index *Expanded*. From that date, a distinction between "SCI" and "SCIE" journals is void — there is no SCI to belong to. Yet the honorific "SCI-class" survived in regulations, evaluation forms, and CVs for years afterward, grading papers against an index that no longer existed. The lesson is general: a hierarchy can outlive its own definition, because the people using a proxy rarely re-read it.

### 2.2 June 2023 — the impact factor floods the waiting room

With the 2023 release of the Journal Citation Reports, Clarivate extended the impact factor to **all** journals in the core collection, ESCI and AHCI included. Until that release, *having* an impact factor at all was the badge of SCIE or SSCI membership; after it, the badge means only "indexed somewhere in the core collection." Every rule of thumb calibrated before June 2023 — "it has an impact factor, so it must be SCIE" — became silently wrong on that date, and much of it is still in circulation.

## 3. Exhibit A — The Waiting Room Outranks the Members

One documented case carries the whole argument.

*Computers and Education: Artificial Intelligence* (Elsevier, founded 2020) is indexed in **ESCI** — by gate hierarchy, the waiting room. In the June 2026 JCR release, its 2025 impact factor was reported at **23.4**, placing it in **Q1 of every category it is listed in**, above nearly every senior-index journal of its field. (The figure was re-verified against the June 2026 release at the time of writing. Section 5 explains why it is the only absolute impact factor this atlas records.)

Hold both facts at once and the hierarchy contradicts itself. By index membership, the journal has not yet passed the gate. By the very number the gatekeeper publishes, it towers over the journals that did. Either the gate measures something the impact factor does not, or the impact factor measures something the gate does not — and both are offered, by the same curator, as measures of the same journal.

There are ordinary explanations: a young journal waits years for senior-index review regardless of its performance, and a young, fast-moving field inflates citation averages (§1.1 of the standard, the distortion of field size). Both explanations are true, and both concede the point: the journal's position in the hierarchy is measuring **age and administrative process**, and its impact factor is measuring **the field's temperature**. Neither is measuring the papers. Exhibit A is Chapter 1 compressed into one row of a spreadsheet.

## 4. Three Disciplines for the Verdict

When a verdict's verification log cites venue context, three rules apply. Each is a consequence of Chapter 1, Chapter 6, and the record above.

### 4.1 Never the absolute impact factor — only the quartile, within a category

An absolute impact factor is doubly unusable: it decays annually, and it carries the field-size distortion, so it cannot travel between fields at all. The quartile within the journal's own category is the only form that survives as a comparison, and it must be cited with its source (§1.2) and year. A verdict that writes "impact factor 23.4" has copied a number that is false within a year; "Q1 in its category, 2025 JCR" is at least a dated fact about a comparison.

### 4.2 The field's position first

Before reading any venue hint, establish where the field's literature lives. Educational technology's journals live in SSCI, not SCIE; expecting an SCIE listing there is a category error by the judge, not a deficiency of the journal. The same error occurs wherever a judge carries one field's index family into another — the venue-hint analogue of the axis-translation rule in Chapter 2 of the standard.

### 4.3 A watchlist is a floor, not a grade

Presence on a credible watchlist voids the venue hint entirely: the paper is then judged as if unpublished, which this standard, alone among the instruments in this atlas, is able to do. Absence from every watchlist certifies nothing. The negative family (§1.5) produces exactly one bit of information, and only in one direction.

## 5. What This Map Is Not

Two classes of knowledge were deliberately excluded from this atlas. Recording the exclusions, with reasons, is what keeps them excluded.

**Not a submission guide.** The working sessions from which this atlas was distilled also contained procedural knowledge of publishing: desk-rejection timelines (about a week), median times to a first decision (about 190 days), cascade submission strategies, advice on carving journal articles from a dissertation, records of where drafts were posted. All of it is excluded. It is knowledge of *how to pass a gate*, and this repository measures *what passed*. The standard is a map drawn after publication (Prohibition 2); its companion cannot be a gate-running manual. If a future contribution proposes to add submission strategy to this repository, this paragraph is the standing reason to decline it.

**Not a handbook of numbers.** Lists of individual journals' current impact factors were also available, and none appear here, with the single dated exception of Exhibit A. A handbook's facts decay yearly; a map keeps only the facts whose decay is itself the point. The 2020 merge, the 2023 flood, and Exhibit A are events fixed in the record with their dates. A table of this year's impact factors would begin to poison this document twelve months from now. Index membership and quartiles move slowly enough to cite with a year attached; absolute citation numbers do not.
